The ink has to sink in

The single thing that separates convincing paper from a beige rectangle is multiply. Ink is not a layer sitting on top of a sheet — it's a stain in it. Composite your type normally and the grain dies underneath it. Composite it with mix-blend-mode: multiply and the fibers show straight through the letterforms.

Every mark on this page multiplies: the type, the rules, the coffee ring, the highlighter, and anything you draw yourself. Nothing here is opaque.

Nothing is straight

Real handwriting has no repeated glyphs. A font does — the third e in a paragraph is pixel-identical to the first, and your eye catches it instantly even if you can't say why. So every glyph on this page is pushed off its outline by a feDisplacementMap, and every block is rotated a fraction of a degree off true.

Nothing on this page is at exactly zero degrees. The sheet, the rules, the sticky note, each heading — all of it sits a little crooked, because a hand put it there.

Pressure, not thickness

A stroke drawn with a constant lineWidth reads as a computer line, always. Real nibs swell where the hand slows and thin out where it accelerates. So the pad below tracks pointer velocity and renders each stroke as a chain of filled quads with a width that breathes — plus real stylus pressure if you have a tablet plugged in.

What's actually on this page

  • Grain, fibers and a raking light — three stacked turbulence layers, rendered once, cached as images.
  • A deckled edge — the one this is named after — and a torn bottom, both displacement maps rather than images.
  • Headings that wipe on the way a hand crosses a page, left to right.
  • A variable-width ink engine with four nibs, running on pointer velocity.
